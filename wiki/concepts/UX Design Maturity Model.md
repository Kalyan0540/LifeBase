# UX Design Maturity Model

A framework developed by [[Jared Spool]] describing the five stages an organisation moves through as design capability grows. Parallels the individual skill-acquisition stages (unconscious incompetence → conscious incompetence → conscious competence → unconscious competence), applied to the organisation as a whole.

## The five stages

1. **Dark Ages** — no one is thinking about design. The organisation is unconsciously incompetent. Products are built without regard for user experience.

2. **Spot UX** — a design-aware individual or project appears. One good project gets acclaim. But the organisation as a whole doesn't understand what happened, and eventually the system reverts — the instigating designer often leaves frustrated.

3. **UX as a Service** — a centralised design team forms, grows, gets funded and gets a "seat at the table." Long considered the end-game. Problem: creates bottlenecks, teams lose institutional memory as designers rotate, product teams resent the dependency.

4. **Embedded UX** — product teams get their own dedicated designers. Full attention, better continuity, less overhead. Considered for a while to be the end-game.

5. **Infused UX** — non-designers (developers, PMs, legal, executives) become design-literate and fluent enough to make smart design decisions on their own. The whole organisation produces better outcomes. This is the actual end-game.

## The UX tipping point

The organisational inflection where the criterion for shipping changes from "works technically + meets business needs" to "works technically + meets business needs + *only if it's designed well*." Companies that reach this tipping point produce consistently delightful products. Companies below it produce products that work but frustrate.

## The maturity key: the least mature influencer

Counter-intuitively, a team's design maturity is set by the maturity of its *least mature influencer* — not the most skilled or average. That person drags all decisions down to their level. The primary job of design leaders is therefore literacy and fluency education for the weakest links, not pixel-level craft.

## Three plays for moving up the maturity ladder

1. **Immersive exposure** — every influencer needs at least 2 hours every 6 weeks watching real users use the product. This alone begins shifting design literacy.
2. **Shared experience vision** — a concrete, vivid story of what using the product will feel like 5 years from now. Everyone marches toward the same flag; decisions are evaluated by asking "which option gets us closer?"
3. **Culture of continuous learning** — daily standup question: "What's the most important thing you've learned and how has it changed what you do?" Reflection accelerates growth at every stage.

## The Disney case

Disney Parks went from stage 1 (the terrible 1997 website) to stage 5 (the $1 billion Magic Band project) in 17 years. Nest started at stage 5 from day one because founder Tony Fadell recruited his entire initial team from Apple's iPod/iPhone design group — people already at mastery. Honeywell, despite owning the thermostat market, could not build the Nest because after Henry Dreyfuss's spot UX project, they developed no sustained design capability.

## Market stage connection

Technology → Feature → Experience → Commodity market stages map directly onto the maturity model. You cannot reach the experience and commodity stages without being close to design-infused. Wi-Fi speed on planes is now a commodity expectation; customers book flights based on it.
