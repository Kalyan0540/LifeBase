# Cancer as a Mitochondrial Metabolic Disease

Cancer as a mitochondrial metabolic disease is the view that cancer starts from damaged cellular energy production rather than primarily from DNA mutations. In [[Thomas Seyfried]]'s version of the model, damaged mitochondria force cells to compensate through fermentation and become dependent on glucose and glutamine.

This is a source-specific medical theory, not standard care guidance.

## Model

The concept has four parts:

- mitochondria normally regulate efficient energy production and cell behaviour
- chronic mitochondrial damage reduces oxidative phosphorylation
- cells compensate with fermentation pathways
- dysregulated growth follows because the cell is now driven by glucose and glutamine metabolism

In this model, genetic mutations can still matter, but they are often treated as downstream damage rather than the first cause.

## Fuel dependency

The source argues that cancer cells cannot reliably switch to fatty acids or ketones because they need functional mitochondria to use those fuels well. That creates a proposed therapeutic vulnerability:

- reduce glucose availability through fasting-like or ketogenic metabolic pressure
- keep normal cells supported by ketones
- target glutamine metabolism separately
- combine the metabolic strategy with standard therapies under clinical supervision

## Press-pulse strategy

Seyfried calls the combined approach "press-pulse":

- **Press** — sustained metabolic pressure, usually by lowering glucose availability and raising ketones
- **Pulse** — targeted interventions against remaining cancer metabolism, especially glutamine use

The source frames this as a way to make chemotherapy, radiation, immunotherapy, repurposed drugs, or hyperbaric oxygen more selective and less toxic. That claim needs clinical validation and medical supervision.

## Prevention frame

For prevention, the source treats cancer risk as part of broader mitochondrial health. Inputs named in the source include diet quality, exercise, sleep, stress reduction, and lower exposure to mitochondrial toxins.

The practical prevention idea is not one diet. It is avoiding long-term residence in a high-glucose, low-ketone, low-activity, high-stress metabolic state.

## Limits

The model is useful as a retrieval concept for Seyfried's work, but it should not be used as treatment advice. The National Cancer Institute's public guidance remains more cautious: popular diets are not proven to treat, cure, or prevent cancer recurrence, though ketogenic diets are being studied in some cancer settings.
