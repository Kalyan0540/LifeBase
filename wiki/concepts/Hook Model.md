# Hook Model

A four-step framework developed by [[Nir Eyal]] for designing products that form user habits. Published in his book *Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products* (2014).

> A hook is an experience designed to connect the user's problem to your product with enough frequency to form a habit.

A **habit** is a behaviour done with little or no conscious thought. Roughly half of all daily behaviour is habitual. The Hook Model describes how to engineer that unconscious pull.

## Step 1 — Trigger

**External triggers**: environmental cues (notifications, ads, buttons) that embed the action in the trigger itself. Product designers know these well.

**Internal triggers**: memories and emotional associations stored inside the user. The most powerful and the most overlooked. Internal triggers are almost always **negative emotions** — boredom, loneliness, uncertainty, fatigue. The product relieves the emotion. Examples:
- Loneliness → Facebook
- Uncertainty → Google
- Boredom → YouTube, Reddit
- Desire for completion → Email

Habits are formed when the internal trigger reliably fires the hook cycle with sufficient frequency (at least once per week; daily or intra-daily is far more powerful).

## Step 2 — Action

The simplest possible behaviour done in anticipation of a reward. Based on BJ Fogg's formula: **B = MAT** (Behaviour = Motivation × Ability × Trigger). Product teams over-invest in motivation (testimonials, persuasion). The higher-ROI lever is **ability**: making the action as easy as possible. Six ability factors:
1. Time required
2. Money required
3. Physical effort
4. Brain cycles (cognitive load)
5. Social deviance (norm-breaking)
6. Non-routine (whether it's a new behaviour)

## Step 3 — Variable Reward

See [[Variable Rewards]]. Satisfying the itch is not enough — the reward must leave the user wanting more. BF Skinner showed that variable schedules of reinforcement produce higher response rates than fixed ones. Three types: Tribe (social), Hunt (information/resources), Self (mastery/completion).

## Step 4 — Investment

The user puts something in — data, content, social connections, reputation — in anticipation of a future benefit, not an immediate one. Investments do two things:
1. **Load the next trigger** — sending a message creates a future external trigger (the reply notification)
2. **Store value** — unlike physical goods that depreciate with use, habit-forming products *appreciate* with use. Stored value makes the product better, more personalised, and harder to leave.

## The Monopoly of the Mind

> "It's not the best product that wins. It's the product that captures the Monopoly of the Mind — the thing we turn to first with little or no conscious thought."

Investment creates switching costs not through explicit lock-in but through accumulated stored value. A new entrant cannot replicate what the user has invested.

## Ethical note

The Hook Model is a tool for changing behaviour. Eyal distinguishes between using it to help users improve their lives vs. exploiting psychological vulnerabilities. His follow-up book *Indistractable* addresses how to resist habit-forming products that work against users' own interests.
