# MAYA Principle

**Most Advanced Yet Acceptable.** A design philosophy developed by Raymond Loewy (1893–1986), the father of industrial design, describing the optimal balance between innovation and familiarity in products, designs, and ideas.

> "The adult public's taste is not necessarily ready to accept the logical solutions to their requirements if the solution implies too vast a departure from what they have been conditioned into accepting as the norm." — Raymond Loewy

## The tension MAYA resolves

People experience two competing drives:
- **Neophilia** — attraction to new things
- **Neophobia** — fear of things that are too unfamiliar

The sweet spot — the MAYA zone — is the most advanced design that sits within the user's current acceptance range. Go below it and you fail to excite; go above it and you alienate.

## The practical formula

> To sell something familiar, make it surprising.  
> To sell something surprising, make it familiar.

## Loewy's track record

Loewy's designs endure because he consistently hit the MAYA zone across wildly different categories: Air Force One livery, Coca-Cola bottle, Shell Oil logo, US Postal Service logo, Greyhound logo, the 1950 Studebaker, modern trains. All felt cutting-edge when released; all felt natural enough to be embraced immediately.

## Applications

**Apple iPod → iPhone progression**: Apple walked users through successive iterations of the iPod — gradually removing buttons, refining the touch interface — across 6 years before launching the iPhone (touchscreen only, no buttons). By 2007, users were ready because the MAYA principle had moved their acceptance boundary incrementally.

**Star Wars**: wildly novel world-building (Jedis, the Force, space opera) wrapped in the oldest narrative structure known to humanity (Campbell's monomyth / hero's journey). Familiar structure made the surprising world acceptable.

**Google Glass failure**: too alien in appearance. No familiar product category to anchor user expectations. Failed the MAYA test in the direction of "too advanced."

**iPhone vs Google Glass**: iPhone felt surprisingly familiar because it was framed as an evolution of the iPod. Google Glass felt like a prototype from the future.

## Connection to other frameworks

- **[[Kano Model]]**: Excitement generators must land within the MAYA zone. A feature that's too unfamiliar becomes alienating rather than delightful.
- **[[First Principles Thinking]]**: MAYA sets the *delivery ceiling* for innovations derived through first principles. You may find a solution that's technically superior but too far from users' current mental models to accept — MAYA tells you to walk them there gradually.
- **[[Delta 4 Framework]]**: A product could be Delta 6 in efficiency terms but fail the MAYA test if the behavioural shift required is too unfamiliar.
