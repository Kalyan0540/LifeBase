# Hotelling's Model

A game theory model of spatial competition that explains why competing businesses cluster together rather than distributing to maximise customer convenience. Formalised by economist Harold Hotelling. Explains the phenomenon of gas stations, coffee shops, fast food chains, and mobile phone kiosks all appearing at the same location.

## The ice cream beach model

Two vendors on a one-mile beach selling identical products. The **socially optimal** arrangement: each at the quarter-mile mark of their half of the beach — no customer walks more than a quarter mile.

But this arrangement is unstable. Either vendor can move toward the centre and capture the middle customers while retaining their end customers. This triggers a competitive repositioning cycle. The stable endpoint — the **Nash Equilibrium** — is both vendors at the centre, back-to-back.

## Nash Equilibrium

The point where neither player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing strategy. In the ice cream case: both at centre. Either vendor who moves toward an endpoint immediately loses the middle to the competitor. So neither moves. The equilibrium is stable — but socially suboptimal (end customers now walk further).

## Why the gap between optimal and equilibrium

- **Socially optimal**: minimises the maximum distance any customer must walk
- **Nash Equilibrium**: maximises each vendor's share given competitive pressure

Individual rational behaviour produces a collectively worse outcome. Classic game-theory tension.

## Real-world applicability

In the real world, businesses compete on more than location — marketing, product differentiation, pricing all complicate the pure model. But at the baseline, Hotelling's prediction holds: competitive pressure drives convergence toward the median customer, not distribution toward optimal customer coverage.

Applied to politics: Hotelling's model predicts why two-party political systems converge toward the centre — each party migrates toward the median voter, producing ideological convergence even when voters would be better served by genuine differentiation.
