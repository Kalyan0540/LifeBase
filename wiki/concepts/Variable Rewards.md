# Variable Rewards

Step 3 of [[Nir Eyal]]'s [[Hook Model]]. The principle that rewards given on a variable schedule produce stronger and more persistent behaviour than fixed-schedule rewards — and that habit-forming products exploit this to keep users coming back.

## The Skinner origin

BF Skinner's operant conditioning experiments: pigeons pecking a disc. Fixed schedule (peck → reward every time) trains a predictable behaviour. Variable schedule (peck → reward sometimes, unpredictably) dramatically *increases* the rate of pecking. The variability itself creates a "wanting" — a desirous anticipatory state. The brain's reward system spikes not just at receiving the reward, but at the uncertainty of whether it will come.

## Why this works neurologically

Variability creates suspense. The brain releases dopamine in anticipation of a possible reward, not just at receipt of a confirmed one. Uncertainty amplifies desire. This is the same mechanism underlying gambling, slot machines, and the infinite scroll.

## Three types of variable rewards

### 1. Rewards of the Tribe
Things that feel good, have element of variability, and come from *other people*: social validation, competition, cooperation, romance, empathetic joy. Social media feeds are the canonical example — you never know what someone posted, how many likes you'll get, what the comments say. Every refresh is a pull of the slot machine lever.

### 2. Rewards of the Hunt
Our primal drive for food and material resources, translated to information search. The feed itself is a hunt: scroll until you find something interesting. The next story *might* be the interesting one, so you keep scrolling. LinkedIn, Twitter, Reddit, YouTube autoplay — all implement the hunt reward. The slot machine is the pure form: variable financial reward with maximum anticipation.

### 3. Rewards of the Self
Intrinsically pleasurable things — mastery, competency, consistency, control, completion — that have variability and don't depend on other people or material resources. Gameplay is the clearest example (getting to the next level). Email is the everyday example: clearing unread messages, completing the to-do list, removing that notification badge. The variable part is not knowing what you'll find when you open the inbox.

## The design implication

Not enough to satisfy — the reward must leave users wanting more. The best habit-forming products satisfy the immediate itch while creating a new itch for the next session.
