# PDC 1996 Keynote with Douglas Adams

[[Douglas Adams]] delivers the keynote at Microsoft's Professional Developers Conference in 1996. A philosophical and comedic meditation on how humans model reality, the nature of data perception, and the relationship between virtual and physical worlds — illustrated through anecdotes, scientific curiosities, and the famous biscuit story.

## The core thesis: we live in models, not reality

Humans cannot process all incoming data, so they maintain a **mental model** of the world and use incoming data to update it rather than perceive the world directly. Evidence:

- **The blind spot** — every eye has a point where the optic nerve attaches, producing a gap in vision. We don't see a black hole because our brain fills in the model. Charles II used to look at courtiers using their blind spot to see them "without heads."
- **The eyeball experiment** — a screen full of gibberish, but sensors on the eye substitute the right letter from *Hamlet's* "To be or not to be" wherever the eye moves. The subject sees only Shakespeare and never notices the gibberish. The model is what we experience, not the screen.
- **The dolphin calibrating human hearing** — researchers thought they were training a dolphin to make a noise for a fish reward. The dolphin was systematically varying pitch and volume to map the researchers' hearing range. The experimenters were the ones being studied.

## Virtual worlds and real worlds

When virtual and physical worlds collide, sign-error bugs become catastrophic: a fighter plane designed and tested entirely in computer simulation flew perfectly until four planes crossing the equator simultaneously flipped over — a sign error in the navigation system that the virtual world had never exposed.

## The biscuit story

The talk's centrepiece: Adams sat in a train station buffet with his newspaper, coffee, and biscuits. A stranger repeatedly took biscuits from what Adams assumed was his own packet. Adams, in British fashion, said nothing — though increasingly aggrieved. Only when he picked up his newspaper to leave did he discover his own, unopened biscuit packet underneath. The stranger had been eating from *his own* packet. Adams had been the thief. The story perfectly illustrates the mental model thesis: we are so committed to our model of the world that contradicting evidence is either ignored or assimilated into the existing narrative.

## Relevance

Adams's ideas connect to [[First Principles Thinking]] — the discipline of questioning the model we carry rather than acting on unexamined assumptions. The story of assumptions blocking perception is a comedic version of the same problem that makes innovation hard: we optimise within the model rather than questioning the model itself.
