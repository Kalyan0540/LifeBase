# How to Build Habit-Forming Products — Nir Eyal (Short)

A 20-minute conference version of [[Nir Eyal]]'s [[Hook Model]] talk. Covers the same four steps as the long version but more concisely, with a focus on practical questions founders should be able to answer about their product.

## Key framing

The one reason anyone uses any product: **to modulate mood** — to feel something different. Products that reliably relieve negative emotional states build habits.

Habit-forming potential is also a function of **frequency**: if the key behaviour does not occur within a week's time or less, it is nearly impossible to form a habit. Daily or intra-daily usage (checking a home screen 150 times a day) offers the highest habit potential.

## The Hook (summary)

1. **Trigger** — external (ads, notifications) vs. internal (emotions). Internal triggers are the critical long-term driver. You must identify the specific negative emotion (the "itch") your product relieves.
2. **Action** — the simplest behaviour done in anticipation of reward. Reduce friction (time, money, effort, brain cycles, social deviance, non-routine) rather than adding motivation.
3. **Variable Reward** — satisfy the itch but leave the user wanting more. Three types: tribe (social), hunt (information/resources), self (mastery/completion). Variability spikes the brain's reward system.
4. **Investment** — user puts in data, content, followers, or reputation. Loads the next trigger; stores value that makes the product better and harder to leave.

## The five diagnostic questions

By the end of the talk Eyal frames the model as five questions every habit-product builder must answer:

1. What is the internal trigger — the frequently-occurring itch your product addresses?
2. What is the external trigger prompting the user to action?
3. What is the simplest behaviour done in anticipation of reward?
4. Is the reward fulfilling yet leaves the user wanting more?
5. What bit of work (investment) increases the likelihood of the next pass through the hook?

## The myth of the best product

> "It's not the best product that wins. It's the product that captures the Monopoly of the Mind — the thing we turn to first with little or no conscious thought."

Investment is what creates switching costs not from explicit lock-in but from stored value that makes the incumbent better than any newcomer, regardless of feature quality.
