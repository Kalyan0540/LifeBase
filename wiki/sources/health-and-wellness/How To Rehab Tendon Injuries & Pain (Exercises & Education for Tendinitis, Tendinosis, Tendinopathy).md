# How To Rehab Tendon Injuries & Pain

An E3 Rehab educational video that gives a detailed framework for tendon pain, terminology, pain monitoring, load management, staged rehab, and limits of passive treatments. It expands the existing tendon load-management notes with more structure and clearer progression rules.

## Terminology

The source explains that persistent tendon pain has moved away from the older labels `tendinitis` and `tendinosis` in many cases.

- `tendinitis` implies acute inflammation, which was often overemphasized historically.
- `tendinosis` implies degeneration, but imaging changes are common even without symptoms and do not always guide treatment.
- `tendinopathy` is presented as the preferred broad term for persistent tendon pain and loss of function related to mechanical loading.

Inflammation may be present, but the source says it usually does not change the primary rehab plan.

## Pain Monitoring

The source uses pain as a dosage signal, not a strict stop sign. A person can often exercise with tolerable pain, but should monitor:

1. whether pain is tolerable during exercise
2. whether pain is better, worse, or the same immediately after exercise
3. whether pain is better, worse, or the same the next day

The next-day response is the most important check because it reveals whether the current dose exceeded recovery capacity.

## Load Management

Tendinopathy is framed as a mismatch between loading and capacity. The common boom-bust cycle is:

1. spike activity
2. symptoms increase
3. rest completely until pain drops
4. return with less activity but still flare
5. repeat until function shrinks

The alternative is to keep some tolerable loading, reduce aggravating frequency/intensity/volume when needed, and track functional progress even if pain is not linearly decreasing.

## Systemic Drivers

Sleep, stress, nutrition, alcohol, smoking, exercise habits, and general well-being can influence symptom onset and persistence. The source does not replace tendon loading with lifestyle change, but treats systemic health as part of the overall recovery environment.

## Rehab Framework

The staged model is presented as overlapping categories rather than rigid steps:

1. isometrics
2. heavy slow resistance
3. energy storage and release
4. return to sport or preferred activity

Isometrics are useful because range of motion, intensity, and loading speed are easy to control. Heavy slow resistance builds capacity through controlled high load. Faster movements and plyometrics are added later when they match the person's goals. Return to sport requires gradual exposure to the actual activity.

The source emphasizes guidelines over universal rules: programming should match the tendon, sport, goals, symptoms, and recovery response.

## Passive Treatments

Foam rolling, massage, icing, or similar adjuncts may be low risk, but they are not the focus because they do not add much beyond exercise-based rehab. The source is cautious about injections: it says PRP evidence is not promising for some tendon problems, and corticosteroid injections may help short-term pain while creating worse long-term outcomes.
