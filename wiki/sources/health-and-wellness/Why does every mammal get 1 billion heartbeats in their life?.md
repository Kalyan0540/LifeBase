# Why does every mammal get 1 billion heartbeats in their life?

Veritasium explainer on biological scaling laws, especially how metabolism, heart rate, lifespan, and body size relate through power laws. The video also extends the same scaling mindset to cities and ends with a caution that the exact universal exponent for metabolism remains debated.

## Core idea

Life does not scale linearly. Many biological properties follow power laws, where doubling size does not simply double every other trait.

The source uses the Tusko elephant LSD dosing case to show the danger of linear mass-based reasoning. A thousand-times-larger animal does not process drugs a thousand times faster because metabolic rate scales sublinearly with mass.

## Metabolic scaling

The surface law predicts metabolic rate should scale with mass to the two-thirds power because heat loss is tied to surface area.

Kleiber's Law instead observes an exponent near three-quarters for many animals: larger animals use more total energy, but less energy per unit mass or per cell than smaller animals.

The source treats this as a major empirical pattern, while also noting that recent data and expert debate leave open whether there is one universal exponent across all life.

## WBE theory

West, Brown, and Enquist proposed that quarter-power biological scaling emerges from resource-distribution networks such as blood vessels.

Their theory assumes:

- delivery networks are space-filling
- terminal units such as capillaries are roughly size-invariant
- evolution pushes transport networks toward efficiency

Because branching biological networks are fractal-like and space-filling, WBE theory derives the three-quarter metabolic exponent and many related exponents for traits such as aorta radius and lung area.

## Heartbeats and lifespan

Heart rate scales approximately with mass to the negative one-quarter power, while lifespan scales approximately with mass to the positive one-quarter power.

Multiplying heart rate by lifespan causes those terms to cancel, leaving a roughly constant lifetime heartbeat count for many mammals: about one billion.

Humans are a major outlier. The source argues that sanitation, germ theory, medicine, and technology have pushed average human lifetime heartbeats closer to three billion.

## Cities

The source then compares biological scaling with city scaling:

- infrastructure such as gas stations, roads, and electrical cables scales sublinearly, so larger cities need less infrastructure per person
- wages, GDP, patents, crime, disease, and some social outputs scale superlinearly, so larger cities intensify both benefits and harms
- walking speed increases with city size, which gives a quantitative basis for the feeling that large-city life is faster

Unlike WBE theory for biology, the source says there is no equally accepted theory explaining city exponents yet.

## Important caveat

The source does not present quarter-power scaling as settled for every organism. It notes expert disagreement about data quality, measurement difficulty, and whether small mammals, large mammals, birds, cold-blooded animals, and other groups share the same exponent.

The durable lesson is not "three-quarters is always correct." It is that many real systems depart from proportionality, so size changes can create efficiency gains, hidden costs, or nonlinear risk.
