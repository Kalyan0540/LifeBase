# Leading Cancer Researcher: They’re Ignoring My Research

A *Diary of a CEO* interview with [[Thomas Seyfried]] about his metabolic theory of cancer. Seyfried argues that cancer should be understood primarily as a mitochondrial metabolic disease rather than only as a genetic disease, and that cancer management should target tumor fuel use through metabolic pressure plus carefully timed standard therapies.

This source is medically high-stakes and contains claims that are not presented as standard oncology guidance. The source itself says the conversation is informational and not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment.

## Core thesis

Seyfried's central claim is that many cancers begin with chronic mitochondrial damage. In his model:

- healthy mitochondria use oxygen efficiently to produce ATP
- damaged mitochondria fall back on older fermentation pathways
- cancer cells become dependent on glucose and glutamine
- DNA mutations are often downstream effects of mitochondrial dysfunction rather than the original cause

He connects this view to Otto Warburg's earlier work on cancer metabolism and argues that mainstream oncology over-centres the somatic mutation theory.

## Mitochondria and chronic disease

The interview presents mitochondria as the control layer for cellular energy, cell behaviour, and broader metabolic health. Seyfried says chronic stressors can damage mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation over time.

Stressors named in the source include:

- highly processed carbohydrates and chronic overfeeding
- inactivity
- chronic emotional stress
- poor sleep
- chronic inflammation
- intermittent hypoxia, such as sleep apnea
- carcinogens, microplastics, forever chemicals, heavy metals, pesticides, and some viral exposures

The source links these exposures to cancer by saying they can damage mitochondrial structure and function, forcing cells into compensatory fermentation.

## Glucose and glutamine

The interview repeatedly names glucose and glutamine as the two main fuels driving cancer-cell fermentation in Seyfried's framework.

The proposed logic is:

- ketogenic or fasting-like states reduce glucose availability
- normal cells can use ketones because their mitochondria remain functional
- cancer cells cannot effectively use ketones because their mitochondria are structurally and functionally impaired
- glutamine remains abundant, so diet alone does not remove both fuels
- metabolic cancer therapy must therefore restrict glucose and also target glutamine metabolism

Seyfried uses the phrase "press-pulse" for this general strategy: press glucose availability down with nutritional ketosis or fasting-like metabolic pressure, then pulse against glutamine metabolism with drugs or other therapies.

## Glucose Ketone Index

Seyfried presents the [[Glucose Ketone Index]] as a quantitative marker for metabolic state. It compares blood glucose and blood ketones after converting glucose into the same unit basis as ketones.

The source's chart frames high GKI zones as higher-risk metabolic states, yellow/green zones as prevention-supportive states, and lower green zones as therapeutic management zones. ## Therapy stance

Seyfried does not argue for abandoning chemotherapy, radiation, or immunotherapy. His claim is that standard therapies should be used differently:

- put the patient into nutritional ketosis or a low-GKI state first
- use lower doses where clinically appropriate
- reduce glucose/glutamine-driven tumor resistance
- combine metabolic pressure with standard-of-care treatments, immunotherapy, hyperbaric oxygen, or repurposed drugs under clinician supervision

The source gives examples involving glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, breast cancer, and mouse models. It also highlights one case of Pablo Kelly, who reportedly lived around 10 years after an inoperable glioblastoma diagnosis while using metabolic therapy and later surgeries.

## Prevention stance

For prevention, Seyfried frames the goal as keeping mitochondria healthy rather than following one named diet. The practical inputs named are:

- avoid living on ultra-processed carbohydrates and refined sugar
- exercise regularly
- protect sleep
- reduce chronic stress
- support access to healthier food
- reduce exposure to mitochondrial toxins where possible
- use GKI or glucose/ketone monitoring only as a personal tool, not as a forced policy

He explicitly rejects government coercion over diet and frames the chart as patient empowerment.

## Medical-context note

Official cancer sources are more cautious than the interview. The National Cancer Institute says popular diets and supplements have not been proven to slow, cure, or prevent cancer recurrence, and people should talk with their doctor before starting a diet or supplement. NCI also notes ketogenic diets are being studied in some cancer contexts, including glioblastoma.

The American Cancer Society's 2026 statistics center estimates 2,114,850 new US cancer cases and 626,140 cancer deaths in 2026, roughly 5,790 new cases and 1,720 deaths per day.
