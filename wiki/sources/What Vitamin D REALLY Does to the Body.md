# What Vitamin D REALLY Does to the Body

Institute of Human Anatomy explainer on vitamin D physiology: how UVB light, skin, liver, kidneys, parathyroid hormone, calcium absorption, and bone health fit together.

## Core idea

Vitamin D is unusual because the body can synthesize it through sun exposure, and because its active form behaves more like a hormone than a simple dietary vitamin.

The source separates the real physiology from broad online claims:

- Vitamin D is important and low levels are common.
- It is not accurate to say everyone is deficient.
- Many claims around immunity, mood, muscle function, hormones, and cancer biology are active research areas, not settled universal benefits.

## Vitamin D3 creation

Vitamin D3, also called cholecalciferol, can come from:

- UVB exposure in the skin
- diet, especially fatty fish, fish oils, and fortified foods
- supplementation

In the skin, UVB light reaches the epidermis and converts 7-dehydrocholesterol into vitamin D3.

Risk factors for lower skin production include:

- short winter days and less time outside
- indoor lifestyle, especially among older adults
- aging skin, which converts vitamin D3 less efficiently
- darker skin pigmentation, because melanin reduces UVB penetration into the epidermis

## Activation pathway

Vitamin D3 is not the fully active form. It needs two major conversion steps:

1. The liver converts vitamin D3 into 25-hydroxycholecalciferol.
2. The kidneys convert that into 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol under the influence of parathyroid hormone.

Doctors commonly measure 25-hydroxycholecalciferol because it is the major circulating form and remains in the blood longer than the fully active form.

## Calcium and bones

The active form of vitamin D helps the small intestine absorb calcium by increasing calcium-binding proteins in intestinal cells.

Without adequate active vitamin D, calcium absorption drops. If blood calcium levels fall, the body still has to preserve calcium for muscle and nervous system function, so it can pull calcium from bone tissue.

Prolonged deficiency can contribute to:

- bone pain
- fractures
- osteoporosis
- osteomalacia in adults
- rickets in children

## Deficiency and testing

Mild to moderate deficiency may have no obvious symptoms. More prolonged or severe deficiency can include fatigue, generalized muscle aches, muscle weakness, or a vague sense of not feeling right, but these symptoms are non-specific.

Routine screening for asymptomatic healthy adults is debated. Testing can be more useful when there are risk factors, symptoms, or clinical reasons to check vitamin D status.

## Supplementation

The source frames modest daily vitamin D3 supplementation, often around 1,000 to 2,000 IU, as a possible maintenance or insurance approach for many healthy adults.

Because vitamin D is fat-soluble, toxicity is possible, though uncommon at modest maintenance doses. Supplementing without testing can help some people, but it is uncertain because the starting blood level is unknown.
