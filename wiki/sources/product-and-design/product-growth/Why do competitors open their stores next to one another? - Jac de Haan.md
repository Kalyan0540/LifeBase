# Why Do Competitors Open Their Stores Next to One Another?

A TED-Ed animation by Jac de Haan explaining [[Hotelling's Model]] of spatial competition and the Nash Equilibrium — why competing businesses cluster together rather than distributing to maximise customer convenience.

## The setup: ice cream on the beach

Two ice cream vendors on a one-mile beach, selling identical products. The socially optimal arrangement is each vendor at the quarter-mile mark of their half — no customer walks more than a quarter mile. Game theorists call this arrangement socially optimal.

## Why the equilibrium doesn't hold

The socially optimal split is *not* a Nash Equilibrium. Either vendor can move toward the centre and steal the middle customers while keeping their end customers. This triggers a cycle of repositioning as each vendor chases the median customer. The stable endpoint — the Nash Equilibrium — is both vendors standing back-to-back at the centre of the beach, each serving exactly 50%.

At this point, neither vendor can improve their position by moving — but the social outcome is worse: customers at the ends of the beach walk further than necessary.

## Nash Equilibrium defined

The point where neither player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing strategy. In the ice cream case: both at centre. Neither benefits from moving because moving toward an endpoint immediately loses the middle to the competitor.

## Real-world applications

- Gas stations clustering at the same intersection
- Fast food chains and clothing boutiques grouping in malls
- Coffee shops on the same corner
- Mobile phone kiosks adjacent to each other

In all cases, customer convenience would be better served by geographic distribution — but competitive pressure drives convergence to the centre.

## The real-world caveat

Real businesses compete on more than location. Marketing, product differentiation, and price cutting all complicate the pure spatial model. But at the heart of the strategy, companies prefer to keep competitors close — confirming the Hotelling prediction as a baseline tendency even when other forces apply.
