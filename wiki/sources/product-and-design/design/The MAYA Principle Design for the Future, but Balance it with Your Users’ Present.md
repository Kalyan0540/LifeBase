# The MAYA Principle

An IxDF article explaining Raymond Loewy's **MAYA Principle** — the design philosophy behind some of the most enduring logos and industrial designs of the 20th century.

## Raymond Loewy

Often called the father of industrial design. Designed: Air Force One livery, Coca-Cola bottle, Shell Oil logo, US Postal Service logo, Greyhound logo, the 1950 Studebaker, the modern train. His track record spans categories as different as cars and pencil sharpeners, over decades.

## MAYA: Most Advanced Yet Acceptable

People are pulled in two directions: **neophilia** (attraction to the new) and **neophobia** (fear of things that are too new). Loewy's insight was to calibrate design to the maximum advance the user's current skill and mental model can accept. Go further and they reject it; stay behind and they're not excited.

> "The adult public's taste is not necessarily ready to accept the logical solutions to their requirements if the solution implies too vast a departure from what they have been conditioned into accepting as the norm."

The practical formula: **to sell something familiar, make it surprising; to sell something surprising, make it familiar.**

## Applications

- **Early iPods** — Apple gradually stripped buttons and refined the interface across multiple generations, making each step small enough to accept. By the time the iPhone launched in 2007 (no buttons, entirely touchscreen), users were ready — they'd been walked there by successive iPod iterations.
- **Star Wars** — Wildly surprising world (Jedis, forces, alien creatures) wrapped in the oldest possible narrative structure (the hero's journey / Campbell's monomyth). The familiar story made the surprising world acceptable.
- **Google Glass** — Lacked MAYA balance in the other direction: too alien in appearance, no familiar product category to anchor it. Users couldn't assimilate it.
- **iPhone** — Reframed as a design update of the iPod. Users already understood the interaction model before picking it up.

## Connection to other concepts

MAYA is closely related to [[First Principles Thinking]] in reverse: first principles helps you identify what's possible; MAYA tells you how much of that possibility users can absorb at once. It's also a lens on the [[Kano Model]] — excitement generators have to hit the MAYA sweet spot to become delightful rather than alienating.
