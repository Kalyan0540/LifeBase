# The Science of Successful Things — Derek Thompson

Derek Thompson, senior editor at *The Atlantic* and author of *Hit Makers: The Science of Popularity in an Age of Distraction*, explains why successful products, stories, and ideas share a particular balance of familiarity and novelty — the MAYA principle applied to popular culture.

## Core thesis

The central question of the book: why do we like what we like? The most interesting answer is the [[The MAYA Principle|MAYA Principle]] — **Most Advanced Yet Acceptable**, Raymond Loewy's theory of design acceptance.

> People are torn between neophilia (they like new things) and neophobia (they're afraid of things that are too new). The genius is combining familiarity and surprise.

## Star Wars as MAYA case study

George Lucas worked on Star Wars for years. The script was widely considered terrible by people he trusted (Francis Ford Coppola among them). The world-building was radically novel — Jedis, the Force, alien species, space opera. He was stuck until he read Joseph Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* — the monomyth: ordinary person → extraordinary journey → defeats nemesis connected to their own origin → returns as hero. Jesus, Buddha, the Odyssey, the Iliad — all the same story.

Lucas took the most surprising world-building exercise he could construct and wrapped it in the oldest, most familiar narrative structure known to humanity. Star Wars sells an incredibly surprising world inside an incredibly familiar story.

## Google Glass vs. iPhone

- **Google Glass**: wildly new product with no familiar category to anchor it. Looked like a prototype. Users rejected it as alien.
- **iPhone**: described by Thompson as essentially a design update of the iPod. Users already understood the interaction model — they'd been trained through successive iPod iterations. The iPhone felt surprisingly familiar for something that had never existed before.

## The practical formula

> To sell something familiar, make it surprising.  
> To sell something surprising, make it familiar.

Starting position matters: if you're building something familiar (a movie sequel, an incremental product), your challenge is to inject surprise. If you're building something radically new, your challenge is to root it in something users already know.
