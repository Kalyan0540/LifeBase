# How To Remember Anything Forever-ish — Notes

Source: Nicky Case’s interactive comic, *How To Remember Anything Forever-ish*.

## Short Summary

The article argues that long-term memory can be trained deliberately through **spaced repetition**: repeatedly testing yourself on information, with the reviews spaced farther apart over time. The core idea is simple but powerful: combine **active recall** with **increasing time gaps**, aim to review just before forgetting, and use small, connected, meaningful flashcards. The result is a practical learning habit that supports creativity, critical thinking, and lifelong learning rather than replacing them.

## Core Message

Memory is not the enemy of creativity or critical thinking. It is one of their foundations. You cannot reason, write, solve problems, improvise, or create well if the relevant building blocks are not available in your mind. Spaced repetition is presented not as a gimmick, but as a way to choose what stays in long-term memory.

The article’s simplest formula is:

> Spaced Repetition = testing + time

In practice, that means you do not merely reread notes. You actively try to recall an answer, check yourself, and then return to the same card later at a carefully spaced interval.

## Why Common Study Methods Fail

The article criticizes several familiar learning habits:

- **Lectures alone** often leave learners passive.
- **Cramming** can create short-term familiarity but does not reliably build durable memory.
- **Rereading** can feel productive while producing weak recall.

The problem is that these methods often create recognition, not retrieval. You may feel that material is familiar, but when tested later, you cannot produce it from memory. Spaced repetition solves this by making retrieval the center of learning.

## The Science Behind Spaced Repetition

### The Forgetting Curve

Hermann Ebbinghaus studied memory experimentally in the 1880s by memorizing nonsense syllables and measuring how quickly he forgot them. His work led to the idea of the **forgetting curve**: after learning something, memory drops quickly at first and then decays more slowly over time if you do not practice recalling it.

The article emphasizes that:

- Most forgetting happens soon after learning.
- Memory decay varies by person and by memory.
- Without recall, memories fade.
- Active recall slows the rate of decay.

### Active Recall Matters More Than Passive Review

Rereading keeps the answer in front of you. Active recall forces your brain to reconstruct it. That effort is what strengthens memory.

A single recall session helps temporarily, but it is not enough by itself. Because forgetting continues, you need repeated recall sessions over time.

### Desirable Difficulty

The article compares memory training to muscle training. If the task is too easy, you do not grow much. If it is too hard, you fail. The best learning happens in the middle: a useful struggle.

This is called **desirable difficulty**.

For spaced repetition, the ideal review time is when you have forgotten a little but not completely. In the article’s phrase, the trick is to **almost forget**. Reviewing too soon wastes effort; reviewing too late means the memory may already be gone.

### Why Review Gaps Should Increase

The best review schedule is not equal gaps, decreasing gaps, or random gaps. It is **increasing gaps**.

Reason:

1. You learn something.
2. Your memory begins to decay.
3. You review it near the “almost forgotten” point.
4. That successful recall slows the future decay rate.
5. Because decay is now slower, it takes longer to reach the same “almost forgotten” point again.
6. Therefore, the next review should happen after a longer gap.

This is the logic behind spaced repetition systems: each successful recall lets you wait longer before reviewing again.

## The Art of Spaced Repetition

The article moves from theory to a concrete system: the **Leitner Box**.

### The Leitner Box

A Leitner Box is a physical flashcard system divided into levels. It works like a game against yourself.

Basic setup:

- Divide a box into levels, such as 7 levels.
- Put all new cards in **Level 1**.
- Review cards according to their level.
- If you get a card right, move it up one level.
- If you get a card wrong, move it back to Level 1.
- If a card reaches the final level and is answered correctly, it can be retired.

### Review Schedule

The article’s basic Leitner schedule doubles the gap at each level:

| Level | Review Gap |
|---|---:|
| Level 1 | Every day |
| Level 2 | Every 2 days |
| Level 3 | Every 4 days |
| Level 4 | Every 8 days |
| Level 5 | Every 16 days |
| Level 6 | Every 32 days |
| Level 7 | Every 64 days |

The principle is more important than the exact number of levels: higher levels mean longer gaps.

### Daily Review Rule

At the end of a daily session, there should be no cards left in Level 1. If a card is still in Level 1, keep testing yourself until you get it right and can move it to Level 2.

### Time Required

The article suggests that a daily session can take roughly **20–30 minutes**. It frames this as an alternative to spending that same time on passive entertainment.

### Start Small

The recommended starting point is:

- Begin with **5 new cards per day**.
- Once that feels comfortable, gradually increase to 10, 15, 20, 25, or 30 cards per day.
- At 30 new cards per day, the article notes that you could learn over 10,000 facts, words, or other items in a year.

The habit is more important than intensity at the beginning. Starting too big can kill momentum.

## Recommended Learning Workflow

Use this as the practical process from the article.

1. **Choose something worth remembering.**  
   Pick a subject connected to something you genuinely care about.

2. **Try to do something real.**  
   Play a song, read a comic in another language, build a project, solve a problem, or have a conversation.

3. **Notice where you get stuck.**  
   The moment of confusion reveals what you actually need to learn.

4. **Look up the missing piece.**  
   Learn the fact, word, concept, technique, or distinction that unblocks you.

5. **Turn it into a small flashcard.**  
   Each card should test one idea only.

6. **Connect the card to context.**  
   Add examples, images, sounds, personal details, use cases, or related facts.

7. **Review with active recall.**  
   Try to answer before checking the back of the card.

8. **Move the card based on performance.**  
   Correct cards move to a longer interval. Incorrect cards go back to the beginning.

9. **Review daily using the same cue.**  
   Attach the habit to a stable moment such as morning, commute, or evening.

10. **Keep improving the cards.**  
   If a card keeps failing, rewrite it to be smaller, clearer, more connected, or more meaningful.

## Making Good Flashcards

The article gives three qualities for effective spaced repetition cards:

> Small, connected, and meaningful.

### 1. Small

A bad card tries to hold too much information. It asks for a paragraph, a whole theory, or a cluster of unrelated details.

A good card asks for one thing.

Examples:

- Bad: “Explain everything about mitochondria.”
- Better: “Mitochondria are the _____ of the cell.”
- Better: “Eukaryotic cells are cells that have what?”
- Better: “What is the most accepted theory for the origin of mitochondria?”

Rule of thumb: **one card, one idea**.

### 2. Connected

Cards become stronger when they link to other cues and contexts.

Useful types of connection:

- Pictures
- Sounds or pronunciation
- Fill-in-the-blank sentences
- Real examples
- Personal memories
- Warnings about common mistakes
- Links to related ideas
- Context where the fact will be used

For language learning, the article shows that a basic “English word → foreign word” card can be improved by adding a sentence, image, pronunciation, grammar clue, and personal connection.

### 3. Meaningful

Spaced repetition works best when it supports something you care about. Do not collect random facts just to feel productive. Memory should serve action.

The article’s preferred pattern is:

1. Try to do something meaningful.
2. Get stuck.
3. Learn what you need.
4. Make a card so you keep that useful knowledge.
5. Return to doing the meaningful thing.

This keeps learning motivated and prevents flashcards from becoming a disconnected pile of trivia.

## Habit Design

The article stresses that spaced repetition is easy mechanically but hard as a daily habit.

To build the habit:

- Pick a consistent time.
- Use the same cue each day.
- Start with a small number of cards.
- Track your streak on a calendar.
- Do not worry if you occasionally miss a day.
- Make the routine feel like a game.

Suggested times:

- After waking up
- During a commute
- Before sleeping
- In the evening

The exact time matters less than consistency.

## Choosing a Tool

The article mentions several tools.

### Leitner Box

Pros:

- Physical and tactile
- Simple to understand
- Good for arts-and-crafts style learners
- Makes the schedule visible

Cons:

- Less portable than an app
- Cannot easily include sound
- Less automated than software

### Anki

Pros:

- Open-source
- Large community
- Powerful features
- Uses a more sophisticated scheduling algorithm
- Supports media such as images and audio

Cons:

- Interface may feel less polished or friendly

### TinyCards

The article mentions TinyCards as easy and visually attractive, but it also notes limitations such as deck size and reduced control over whether you got a card right. Note: TinyCards was later discontinued, so Anki or another active tool is more practical today.

### Other Tools Mentioned

- SuperMemo
- Mnemosyne
- NimbleNotes

The article’s point is not that one tool is mandatory. The important principles are active recall, increasing intervals, and well-designed cards.

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Bloated Cards

If a card asks too much, you will avoid it or fail it repeatedly. Split it into smaller cards.

### Pitfall 2: Disconnected Facts

Random facts are harder to remember and easier to abandon. Connect cards to other knowledge, examples, and goals.

### Pitfall 3: Meaningless Collection

Spaced repetition is not about hoarding impressive information. It should serve curiosity, projects, relationships, skills, or life goals.

### Pitfall 4: Passive Reviewing

Looking at the answer and thinking “I knew that” is not enough. You must try to recall first.

### Pitfall 5: Starting Too Big

Adding too many new cards too quickly creates a review burden. Begin with 5 new cards per day and scale only when the habit is stable.

### Pitfall 6: Ignoring Failed Cards

A repeatedly failed card is feedback. It may be too vague, too large, badly worded, or disconnected from meaning.

### Pitfall 7: Treating Memory as Separate From Use

The article argues that the best learning loop is doing → getting stuck → learning → remembering → doing again. If cards never return to real use, they lose value.

## Key Terms

- **Spaced repetition:** Testing yourself on information at expanding intervals.
- **Active recall:** Trying to retrieve an answer from memory before checking it.
- **Forgetting curve:** The pattern that memory fades quickly at first and more slowly later without review.
- **Desirable difficulty:** The productive zone where recall is challenging but possible.
- **Leitner Box:** A flashcard system where cards move through levels with longer review intervals.
- **Increasing gaps:** The review pattern where intervals get longer after successful recalls.
- **Small, connected, meaningful:** The article’s three rules for strong flashcards.

## Concise Takeaways

- Spaced repetition is not just memorization; it is a way to make useful knowledge durable.
- The method works by combining active recall with time.
- Review just before forgetting, not immediately and not too late.
- Successful recalls should create longer gaps before the next review.
- The Leitner Box is a simple physical implementation of this idea.
- Start with 5 new cards a day to protect the habit.
- Good cards are small, connected, and meaningful.
- Learning works best when it supports something you care about doing.
- Memory supports creativity and critical thinking; it does not compete with them.

## One-Sentence Final Reminder

Use spaced repetition to turn learning from a temporary encounter into a durable, personal library of ideas you can actually use.
